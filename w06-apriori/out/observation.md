# Week 6 Observations · Frequent Itemsets & Association Rules

## Task 1 · A-Priori & Association Rules
- **Asymmetric Confidence**: For the pair `{cola, diaper}` (at support 2 in the toy dataset), `confidence(cola -> diaper) = 2/2 = 1.0` (100%), whereas `confidence(diaper -> cola) = 2/5 = 0.4` (40%). The forward direction is the useful actionable insight (anyone buying cola almost certainly buys diapers), whereas the reverse direction is uninformative because diapers are bought frequently with many other household items.
- **Top Rule & Lift**: The highest-confidence rule found is `diaper -> beer` (confidence 0.80, lift 1.12). Despite high confidence, its lift is very close to 1.0 because beer already appears in 5 of 7 baskets (baseline 71.4%). Thus, the rule reflects the baseline popularity of beer rather than a genuine co-purchase affinity.
- **Brute Force vs. A-Priori Counters**: With 2,000 items, brute force must allocate $\binom{2000}{2} = 1,999,000$ pair counters upfront. After pass one on this benchmark dataset, A-Priori identified 1,849 frequent singletons, requiring only **820,259** candidate pair counters in pass two (a **59.0%** reduction in allocated counters).

## Task 2 · Support Explosion Curve
- **The Breaking Point**: Below support $s \approx 25$, the single-machine baseline encounters memory strain: memory consumption rises past 160 MB and runtime increases. On larger real-world datasets with tens of thousands of items, lowering support past this inflection point causes pair counters to exceed available RAM, leading to fatal memory allocation failure (`MemoryError`) or severe OS disk swapping.
- **Counter Growth Rate (A4)**: Halving support from 400 $\to$ 200 increased counters by **5.54×** (10,585 $\to$ 58,626), and 200 $\to$ 100 increased counters by **4.67×** (58,626 $\to$ 273,701). Because pass two counts pairs of surviving items quadratically ($\approx \frac{1}{2}|L_1|^2$), counter growth is significantly worse than doubling per halving until saturating at the total observed distinct pair limit (893,456).
- **Answer vs. Counter Divergence (A5)**: True frequent pairs grew steadily at $\approx 2.5\times - 3.1\times$ per halving (249 $\to$ 776 $\to$ 2,244 $\to$ 6,397 $\to$ 17,045 $\to$ 43,116 $\to$ 101,938 $\to$ 250,054), while counters exploded much faster early on. At support 50, one must track 820,259 counters to find only 6,397 answers—meaning over 99% of counters track transient candidates that will be discarded.

## Task 3 · PCY & Memory Accounting
- **Bucket Count & Hash Design**: Chose $B = 2,000,003$ prime buckets. With ~3.8 million pair occurrences across 20,000 baskets, the average load is $\approx 1.9$ counts/bucket. Because the threshold is 50, non-frequent pairs rarely accumulate enough hash collisions to trigger a bucket, achieving a **98.8% reduction** in peak counters (from 820,259 down to 9,719 counters).
- **Honest Memory Accounting & Crossover (R5)**: During pass 1, the bucket array occupies $2\times 10^6$ integers (~16 MB), but collapses to a compact boolean bitmap (~2 MB) before pass two begins. In contrast, PlainApriori's 820,259 dictionary counters require ~108 MB. Total peak memory dropped from ~108 MB to ~16 MB. The crossover point occurs when the number of candidate pairs in PlainApriori multiplied by dictionary entry size exceeds the fixed memory of the bucket array ($B \times 4\text{ bytes}$). On dense datasets with high candidate density, PCY decisively wins.
- **Degradation with Small Bucket Array**: If $B$ is reduced to 50,000, average bucket load rises to $3.8 \times 10^6 / 50,000 = 76 > 50$. Almost every bucket becomes frequent, the bitmap saturates with 1s, and PCY loses all filtering power, falling back to all 820,259 counters.

## Task 4 · SON Algorithm (Optional)
- **Three-Line Proof of No False Negatives (A6)**:
  1. Let $I$ be an itemset whose total count across all $N$ baskets is at least $s$ ($\sum_{i=1}^k \text{count}_i(I) \ge s$).
  2. If $I$ were infrequent in every chunk $i$, then $\text{count}_i(I) < s \cdot \frac{m_i}{N}$ for all $i = 1, \dots, k$.
  3. Summing across chunks gives $\sum_{i=1}^k \text{count}_i(I) < \sum_{i=1}^k s \frac{m_i}{N} = s \frac{\sum m_i}{N} = s$, which directly contradicts the premise that $I$ is globally frequent. Hence, $I$ must be frequent in at least one chunk.
- **Effect of Changing Chunks (A5)**: When changing `--chunks` from 8 to 16, the number of candidate pairs moved from **14,201 to 24,418** (smaller chunks exhibit higher local variance), but the final frequent pairs count did **not** move (stayed exactly **6,397**).
- **Why SON is Right Despite Being Slower**: On a single machine where all data fits in RAM, PlainApriori and PCY are faster. However, when the dataset is distributed across a cluster or too large for single-node RAM, single-machine algorithms cannot run at all. SON decomposes the problem into independent parallel chunks in pass one and an exact verification in pass two, providing linear horizontal scaling and zero communication during pass one.
- **Pass Two Optimization**: Replaced brute-force candidate-against-basket subset testing ($20,000 \times 14,201 \approx 284\text{M}$ tests, ~12.2s) with candidate-filtered basket combinations and hash set lookups ($20,000 \times 190 \approx 3.8\text{M}$ lookups), reducing runtime to **2.09s** (**5.8× faster**).
